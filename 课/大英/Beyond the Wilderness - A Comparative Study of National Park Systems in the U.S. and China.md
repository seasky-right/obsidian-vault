# Abstract 
This study presents a comparative analysis of the national park systems of the United States and China, focusing on the cultural divergences that shape their distinct conservation models. Drawing on historical, spatial, and experiential dimensions, the strict physical separation between humans and nature enforced by the American framework is examined and contrasted with the Chinese model of “zoned coexistence,” which is informed by the traditional philosophy of harmony between humanity and nature (tianren heyi). In this study, the historical origins, spatial governance strategies, and visitor experiences of the two systems are systematically compared. Ultimately, it is suggested that effective ecological conservation depends not on the universal application of a single model, but on the adaptation of spatial frameworks to specific national and cultural conditions.

**Key words:** National park system, wilderness ideal, zoned coexistence, spatial management, cultural divergence

# Introduction
As the concept of the "National Park" has spread globally over the past century, the term itself has become a universal symbol of nature conservation. However, visitors to Yellowstone in the U.S. and Wuyishan in China encounter two vastly different landscapes. This contrast stems from the deep localization of the originally American conservation framework. It is cultural divergence, rather than pure geographic variety, that gave rise to two fundamentally different spatial philosophies: the American wilderness ideal, which enforces strict separation between humans and nature, and the Chinese model of "zoned coexistence," rooted in the ancient philosophy of "harmony between humanity and nature" (Tianren Heyi). This paper traces how these diverging philosophies emerged, shaped spatial governance, and defined visitor experiences.

# I. Historical Origins of Divergent Models
The American national park model was shaped from the outset by a philosophy of strict human–nature separation. Established in 1872 with Yellowstone, the system drew on 19th-century Romanticism and Transcendentalism, which together recast wilderness as a sacred sanctuary rather than a realm to be feared. The vast landscapes were increasingly viewed as a source of national pride, serving as the American answer to Europe's ancient cathedrals. This feeling was deepened by Transcendentalism, which taught that nature was a pure, untouched sanctuary. Rooted in these ideas, wilderness was seen as an uncorrupted world, providing an essential antidote to industrial civilization (Nash, 2014). However, this "wilderness myth" hides a harsh historical reality. To sustain the perception of a landscape untouched by humans, early park management enforced strict physical separation. This policy directly led to the forced removal of indigenous communities, such as the Shoshone people, from their ancestral lands (Spence, 1999). As a result, the core logic of the American model was set: nature is a sacred temple, and humans are merely temporary visitors who must leave no trace. Though this wilderness ideal was powerful, early park managers often acted more as landscape gardeners than as ecologists, a flaw that later generations would have to correct.

When China introduced the national park framework decades later, it faced conditions that made the American exclusion model unworkable. The immediate need was to solve a long-standing problem of divided management in conservation. This problem largely stemmed from that nature reserves were run by several overlapping departments, each with its own standards. The giant panda habitat, for instance, was long divided by provincial boundaries across Sichuan, Shaanxi, and Gansu, leaving panda populations isolated and protection efforts scattered. The new national park system therefore aimed to break through such administrative barriers and protect the authenticity and integrity of entire ecosystems. However, forced relocation is highly impractical in China due to its high population density and millennia of agricultural settlement. Moreover, large ecological areas like Sanjiangyuan, covering 190,700 square kilometers (State Council of China, 2021), are home to generations of herders and involve complex collective land ownership. For these reasons, a deep adaptation was necessary, and the basic conservation approach shifted from physical exclusion to human-nature coexistence.

# II. Spatial Planning and Management Philosophies
The divergence in philosophical commitments, specifically wilderness preservation versus human-nature integration, directly shapes the distinct spatial planning approaches of the two nations. The U.S. model maintains strict exclusion in its core areas, allowing ecosystems to develop naturally with minimal human presence. For example, Everglades National Park protects over 1.5 million acres of subtropical wilderness where human activity is tightly restricted to preserve the natural flow of water and wildlife (National Park Service, 2023). This approach reflects a belief that nature functions best when left undisturbed. 

Departing from this exclusionary logic, China utilizes a carefully designed "dual-zoning system" to balance ecological protection and human livelihoods. In core protection zones, strict ecological red lines are enforced. For vast unpopulated areas like Hoh Xil, modern technologies are widely used to avoid the need for a large on‑site personnel. Remote sensing (RS) data and unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), for instance, provide efficient and non-invasive monitoring to prevent poaching. With the ecological baseline firmly protected, the general control zones are permitted to host moderate human activities. In Wuyishan National Park, for example, local farmers are allowed to maintain traditional tea gardens. A similar balance is found in Sanjiangyuan, where an innovative "One-Household-One-Post" ranger system has been set up (Ma et al., 2023). This arrangement, closely combining conservation with local livelihoods, not only serves the core objective of preserving ecological wholeness, but also offers a practical solution to the country's complex national conditions, where large populations have lived in and around protected areas for generations.

# III. Visitor Experience and Cultural Values
Beyond management boundaries, visitor experience further reflects these contrasting cultural values. In broad terms, the American model treats nature as a site of individual challenge, while the Chinese model presents it as a shared aesthetic journey.

Valuing individualism, the American system offers deliberately simple facilities and encourages self-reliance. Yosemite National Park's Half Dome trail, for example, requires hikers to apply for a permit, carry their own gear, and ascend steep cables with no guardrails. This arrangement reflects a distinct American belief: facing natural challenges at one's own risk is not neglect by park management, but the highest form of wilderness respect. Accordingly, excessive tourist amenities are often criticized for diluting this authentic backcountry spirit. 

Grounded in a different philosophical tradition, Chinese culture views nature as an extension of human living space and values shared, accessible enjoyment. Parks such as Jiuzhaigou therefore provide well-developed yet low-impact facilities, including extensive wooden boardwalks and a centrally dispatched eco-bus network. These are not considered intrusions on nature, but rather public services that allow citizens of all ages and physical abilities to access and experience the natural environment safely. Therefore, experiencing nature here becomes a shared aesthetic journey rather than a lonely survival test.

# IV. Modern Convergence and Adaptation
In recent decades, both models have undergone their own corrections and gradually begun to converge toward a shared understanding. Within the U.S. system, early park management was driven less by ecological science than by a scenic ideal, which led to questionable interventions. In Yellowstone, for instance, wolves were systematically eliminated to create a safer and more visually pleasing landscape. The resulting ecological imbalance later forced a reversal, and the 1995 wolf reintroduction triggered a trophic cascade that restored the park's health (Boyce, 2018). This shift from scenery management to ecosystem repair reflects a growing American awareness that passive preservation is often insufficient. Building on this, the U.S. National Park Service has also begun to bring back traditional indigenous practices, such as prescribed burning, to maintain forest health and prevent catastrophic wildfires. 

Conversely, in China, the newly established national park system increasingly prioritizes ecological protection, strictly limiting over-commercialization in sensitive areas. What links these two developments is a common recognition: protecting nature well requires both scientific understanding and a willingness to actively manage rather than simply withdraw. This convergence points to a broader consensus that effective conservation depends not on any single fixed model, but on continuous adaptation to ecological realities. However, despite this convergence, the fundamental cultural difference between the two models remains clearly visible: the American model still presumes that core protected areas should be largely free of permanent human settlement, whereas the Chinese model continues to accommodate human communities within its zoned framework.

# Conclusion
Ultimately, the development of national parks in these two countries shows that no single model of conservation fits all situations. By tracing their historical origins, comparing their spatial planning strategies, and examining visitor experiences, it becomes evident that management frameworks are inextricably linked to cultural identities. The American "wilderness" approach seeks to protect nature by excluding human presence, while the Chinese "harmony" model explores a sustainable way of coexistence in areas with dense human populations. Understanding this cultural difference goes beyond simply comparing spatial management techniques. It offers different and valuable wisdom for humanity in facing the serious global environmental challenges ahead.
